2. OCR problem

~3 min
Say

My GRPO runs were not improving text until I tried a run with many more images. First, why the normal runs failed.

Two boxes (green, then orange)
Say

The reward signal is there: inside a group, OCR varies a lot, and most groups contain a good image.

The gradient signal is not. Per image, the useful part of the gradient is about 1/10,000 of the noise. Signal equals noise only at 6,000–10,000 images per update, and we used 256.

Bar chart (gradient share)
  • The last bar is 57% of the gradient, and 92% of the KL penalty's gradient.
Say

The last step has tiny noise, and the log-prob gradient grows like 1 over sigma, so it dominates. But it only sharpens details; letters form in steps 2–5. So I left the last step out and trained an early window.

Signal chart (5 variants)
  • All five intervals overlap zero.
Say

I tested whether each image's own starting noise was the problem, by sharing it within the group. It wasn't: no variant raises the signal. But the 2-step window keeps the same signal and is about 4× cheaper per image. That leaves two fixes: many more images, or a lower-variance gradient. I tried both: first the long run.

4. Fix 2: differentiable OCR

~1 min
Training curves
  • The CLIP text-match term goes up, while PaddleOCR (real legibility) goes down.
Say

The second fix was a lower-variance gradient: I backpropagated a differentiable OCR reward. Even before training, no differentiable reader agreed with PaddleOCR. In training the proxy improved while the real metric halved: reward hacking. On new seeds, text and image quality collapsed. So volume is the route that works.

6. Is the gain real?

~2 min
Table: go row by row, comparing "Base 10" with "Ours 10"
Say

My worry was that the model just writes more text or repeats the word to exploit the reward. So I re-scored the same images with strict OCR, exact line matches, a second reader (TrOCR) and a fully independent engine (EasyOCR). The gain survives every check. 8. Noise judge

~2 min
Say

Now the weaker part: the noise judge on SD1.5.

Warrior image rows
  • Top row: NoisyCLIP's own colour map, made for SDXL. The colours are wrong on SD1.5.
  • Middle row: our linear map Φ on the predicted clean image. The real layout is visible from step 4–6.
Line chart (Spearman per step)
  • x axis: the step where the judge looks. y axis: how well its score predicts the final image's CLIP score.
  • Gray line (the paper's input, zt): near zero.
  • Blue line (NoisyCLIP on Φ, what we use): 0.44 at step 5, no better than plain CLIP on the same view.
  • TAESD (a tiny decoder) is more accurate, but 2.6× more expensive.
Table, column by column
  • Wrong-prompt detection: the judge scores the image with its own prompt and with a random other prompt. NoisyCLIP on Φ picks the right one 100% of the time.
  • Spearman, all images: does the score at step 5 predict the finished image's CLIP score? NoisyCLIP 0.44; TAESD 0.63; VAE 0.67.
  • Within the same prompt: only ranking the 4 images of each prompt against each other, which is what GRPO uses. NoisyCLIP 0.29; TAESD 0.35; VAE 0.32.
  • First row: NoisyCLIP as published (zt + SDXL colour map): −0.09 and 78%, so nothing on SD1.5.
Say

NoisyCLIP easily tells a completely wrong prompt apart. What is hard at step 5 is ranking images of the same prompt: 0.29. No cheap judge does much better that early; even a full VAE decode is at 0.32.

Used as published, it gives nothing on SD1.5. With our Φ view it works like plain CLIP on that view, without the gain noise-aware training should bring. The paper reports about 0.9 midway on SDXL, so off the shelf it doesn't transfer well to SD1.5.

If asked: the paper's 0.9 is at 50 steps on SDXL and ours is at 10 steps on SD1.5, so it is not a side-by-side comparison.

Proposal: StitchVM

~2 min
The "Proposed" box, then the plan
Say

In one sentence: instead of turning the latent into a poor-quality image for NoisyCLIP to judge, we connect SD1.5's internal features straight to NoisyCLIP through a small adapter, so the judge sees from the first steps how the final image will turn out.

We saw that NoisyCLIP doesn't transfer to SD1.5: 0.44 at step 5. The problem is the linear projection: early on the latent is mostly noise, and that is exactly where we need the judge.

So I'd like to try Option B from the plan, StitchVM. Instead of projecting the latent to RGB, it takes the diffusion model's own hidden features and connects them to the reward model through a small adapter. It predicts the final reward from the current state, and the paper reports it beats linear projection at high noise.

StitchVM was built for transformers. SD1.5 is a UNet, so I'd use its down- and mid-block features, which is a new adaptation. Those features are computed anyway, so the judge is almost free.

I'd test it offline first: train only the adapter on trajectories I already have, then compare with NoisyCLIP, TAESD and the VAE on the same images. About a day of work. It goes into GRPO only if it clearly beats NoisyCLIP at steps 3–5.

Hard questions, short answers

  • "Isn't the gain just OCR substring credit?" No. Strict OCR, TrOCR and EasyOCR all show it, and repetition is 0.3%.
  • "Why are the two seeds so different?" Held-out OCR moves ±0.03 between checkpoints, and seed 1's last one landed high. Both are clearly above base 10.
  • "Did alignment get worse?" Alignment PickScore −0.0013 for seed 1, borderline; n.s. for seed 2. Text-prompt PickScore went up.
  • "Is it just more compute?" Yes, volume is the fix: 256k images, about 23 h on one H200. The 2-step window makes each image about 4× cheaper to train on.
  • "Where is the noise judge in the long run?" It scores alignment (weight 100 vs OCR 80) with no VAE decode. The text gain comes from OCR on the final image.
  • "Why not make NoisyCLIP work on SD1.5?" I already gave it our own SD1.5 colour map: 0.44, the same as plain CLIP. The limit is the projection itself.
  • "Does continuing training help?" Training-word OCR flattened; the held-out answer comes later today.
